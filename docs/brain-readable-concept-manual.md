# Brain Readable Concept Manual

Status: compiled readable concept document committed to the Brain repository.

This document is a human-readable guide to the Brain corpus. It is not a narrowed implementation plan, not a replacement for the extraction matrix, and not a claim that the software has been fully built or proven. It is meant to let a reader understand the major ideas, concepts, theories, formulas, modules, runtime loops, dashboards, tests, contradictions, and roadmap stages that have been compiled for the Brain project.

The governing rule is preservation by default. Concepts are not removed because they are speculative, future-stage, theoretical, outside V0, hard to build, contradictory, or not immediately commercial. V0 is only the first proof harness. It is not the total Brain.

---

## 1. What the Brain Is

The Brain is a proposed intelligence operating system that combines commercial intelligence, source monitoring, belief updating, memory, formulas, outcome learning, graph rewiring, attention allocation, trust controls, capital allocation, human-brain-like cognitive architecture, and operator dashboards.

In plain English, the Brain is designed to notice signals in the world, decide which ones deserve attention, form beliefs, predict what may happen, recommend actions, require human approval for external consequences, track what actually happened, and learn which signals created value versus which ones wasted time.

The core loop is:

```text
source -> signal -> evidence -> belief -> opportunity -> buyer/offer/action -> approval -> outcome -> attribution -> reward/pain -> graph update -> reallocation
```

The Brain is not intended to be just a chatbot, dashboard, CRM, scraper, lead-generation tool, or static knowledge base. Those may be components, but the larger concept is an adaptive intelligence system that learns from outcomes and changes how it allocates attention, trust, memory, and capital.

---

## 2. The Big Idea

The Brain watches the world for useful change. A change may come from a source such as a filing, regulation, job post, market listing, procurement signal, message, price movement, social signal, or internal note.

The Brain does not treat every signal equally. It asks:

```text
Is this new?
Is it reliable?
Is it commercially relevant?
Is it urgent?
Does it contradict something we believed?
Does it create a money path?
Does it create trust, legal, reputation, or capital risk?
What would prove or disprove it?
What action could this support?
What happened the last time we saw something like this?
```

When an outcome is logged, the Brain updates its beliefs and pathways. A source, signal pattern, buyer type, offer, or action that repeatedly creates useful outcomes gains weight. One that wastes time, misleads, or causes pain loses weight.

This is why the Brain is called a Brain: the important design is not just data storage. It is perception, attention, memory, learning, inhibition, prediction, action selection, and outcome-driven rewiring.

## 4. Non-Negotiable Rules

### Preserve by default

Material is retained unless there is an allowed reason to reject it, such as exact duplication, corruption, explicit user rejection, legal/safety restriction, or confirmed irrelevance.

### External action is approval-gated

The Brain may analyze, simulate, rank, draft, and recommend. It must not independently send outreach, spend money, make introductions, make legal claims, commit to deals, or publish sensitive conclusions without approval.

### No learning without attribution

A good outcome cannot strongly reinforce a source, action, strategy, formula, or pathway unless the Brain has reasonable attribution that the outcome was caused by that pathway.

### No score without trace

Every score must have formula provenance: which formula was used, what inputs were used, what output was produced, what object it affected, and what decision consequence it had.

### Contradictions are preserved

Contradictions are not deleted. They become review items, investigation triggers, or unresolved questions.

### V0 is not the whole Brain

V0 is the first proof harness. It should prove the core loop. It must not delete later-stage concepts.

## 5. The Brain in One Loop

### Step 1: Source

A source is where information comes from. It may be a registry, filing, court record, procurement site, job board, marketplace, internal note, message, data feed, or research artifact.

The Brain tracks source reliability, freshness, access status, extraction difficulty, false-positive rate, and historical usefulness.

### Step 2: Signal

A signal is a piece of observed change or information. It may be a new filing, price change, job posting, regulation, equipment listing, buyer message, supplier claim, or contradiction.

Signals are scored for novelty, relevance, urgency, commercial upside, source quality, and risk.

### Step 3: Evidence

Evidence is the part of a signal that supports or contradicts a claim. Evidence can be weak or strong, primary or secondary, cheap talk or costly behavior.

The Brain treats a payment, contract, regulator record, procurement notice, or official filing as stronger evidence than a vague claim or promotional post.

### Step 4: Belief

A belief is a live claim with confidence. It is not treated as permanently true. It has supporting evidence, contradicting evidence, dependencies, confidence, and decay.

Example:

```text
Belief: Company X may be preparing for expansion.
Supporting evidence: job postings, facility update, procurement language.
Contradicting evidence: no confirmed budget, no official announcement.
Status: under review.
```

### Step 5: Opportunity

An opportunity is a belief or signal that may support action. It requires a buyer, pain, timing, evidence, a possible offer, and a path to outcome.

### Step 6: Action Recommendation

The Brain may recommend an action such as research, verification, outreach draft, memo, buyer mapping, follow-up, monitor, archive, kill, or escalate.

### Step 7: Approval

External action waits for approval. Approval is the boundary between internal cognition and real-world consequence.

### Step 8: Outcome

An outcome records what happened: reply, no reply, meeting, sale, payment, loss, trust impact, wasted time, false positive, saved time, or risk avoided.

### Step 9: Learning

The Brain compares prediction to outcome, calculates error, assigns attribution, creates reward or pain, updates graph weights, and changes future attention.

### Step 10: Reallocation

The Brain reallocates attention, trust, capital, source priority, sensor priority, memory weight, and strategy emphasis based on what worked.

## 6. How the Brain Pays Attention

Attention is scarce. The Brain cannot deeply process everything. It needs an attention system.

A plain-English attention rule:

```text
Give more attention to signals that are valuable, novel, urgent, reliable, contradictory, close to an active mandate, or high-learning-value.
Give less attention to signals that are noisy, stale, duplicated, low-source-quality, expensive to review, or unlikely to change a decision.
```

A preserved attention formula family is:

```text
attention_score =
  commercial_upside
+ novelty
+ urgency
+ source_quality
+ contradiction_value
+ proximity_to_active_mandates
+ timing_sensitivity
+ learning_value
+ trust_risk_if_ignored
- noise_probability
- stale_context
- duplication
- operator_load_penalty
- fulfillment_burden
```

What this means: the Brain should not chase every interesting thing. It should prioritize signals that are useful, timely, trustworthy, and likely to change action.

## 7. How the Brain Forms Beliefs

The Brain uses belief updating. A belief starts with a prior confidence. New evidence raises or lowers that confidence.

The basic Bayesian form is:

```text
P(H | E) = [P(E | H) × P(H)] / P(E)
```

Plain English:

```text
How likely is the hypothesis after seeing the evidence?
```

The Brain should update strongly when evidence is reliable, costly, timely, corroborated, and relevant. It should update weakly when evidence is vague, promotional, uncorroborated, stale, contaminated, or from a source with incentive to exaggerate.

Contradictions should not be erased. They should be stored and reviewed.

## 8. How the Brain Learns From Outcomes

The Brain learns only when actions and outcomes are linked.

It should ask:

```text
What did we predict?
What actually happened?
What caused the outcome?
Was the source useful?
Was the signal real?
Was the buyer reachable?
Was the offer right?
Was the timing right?
Was the outcome luck, operator judgment, source quality, or the Brain's recommendation?
```

Reward reinforces useful pathways. Pain weakens bad pathways or triggers protection.

Reward examples:

```text
signal led to revenue
signal led to qualified meeting
prediction was correct
contradiction prevented bad action
source produced useful intelligence
action saved time
```

Pain examples:

```text
false lead wasted time
bad entity match
unsupported claim risk
stale source caused wrong conclusion
low-value alert consumed attention
duplicate signal created noise
```

The important rule: high reward should not over-reinforce a source or strategy unless attribution is strong.

## 9. How the Brain Remembers

The Brain needs several memory types.

### Working memory

What the Brain is actively thinking about now. It should be limited so the cockpit does not show everything.

### Episodic memory

Specific events: who, what, when, where, why, what evidence, what action, what outcome.

### Semantic memory

General knowledge and patterns: source reliability, buyer behavior, opportunity patterns, market structures.

### Procedural memory

How to do things: playbooks, workflows, source review procedures, offer validation steps.

### Emotional memory

Reward, pain, trust impact, urgency, risk, surprise, and severe lessons.

### Social memory

Relationship stage, trust balance, credibility gained or spent, follow-ups owed, and reputation risk.

### Consolidation and replay

The Brain should revisit events offline, merge duplicates, extract patterns, decay stale beliefs, surface contradictions, and propose memory updates.

## 10. How the Brain Thinks Ahead

The Brain should simulate before action.

It should ask:

```text
What might happen if we act?
What might happen if we wait?
What would prove this is worth action?
What would disprove it?
What is the cost of more research?
What is the cost of being wrong?
What is the value of information?
What is the regret if we choose wrong?
```

Important concepts include:

```text
expected utility
EVPI: expected value of perfect information
EVSI: expected value of sample information
counterfactual reasoning
causal inference
scenario simulation
dreaming/offline recombination
regret minimization
```

The goal is not endless research. The goal is better decisions with enough evidence.

## 11. How the Brain Handles Risk and Trust

Trust is treated as a scarce resource. A profitable action can still be bad if it damages trust, legal standing, reputation, or long-term access.

The Brain needs:

```text
approval gates
trust-adjusted value
legal risk checks
reputation risk checks
source poisoning detection
prompt injection handling
cognitive immune filtering
budget caps
audit logs
rollback rules
```

A trust-adjusted action rule:

```text
trust_adjusted_value =
  action_expected_utility
- trust_damage_risk
- reputation_damage_risk
- legal_or_access_risk
```

If trust damage is severe, the action should be blocked or escalated even if expected cash value is high.

## 12. Capital, Attention, and Trust Allocation

The Brain should not optimize only for gross upside. It should evaluate risk-adjusted performance.

Plain-English versions of key metrics:

```text
Sharpe-like score: return compared with overall volatility.
Sortino-like score: return compared with downside volatility.
Calmar-like score: return compared with maximum drawdown.
Omega-like score: gains above threshold compared with losses below threshold.
Kelly criterion: how much to risk when edge and odds are known.
Drawdown risk: how much loss and recovery pain a strategy may create.
Opportunity half-life: how quickly an opportunity loses value.
```

The Brain should allocate more to sources, sensors, offers, actions, and strategies that create repeatable value without unacceptable trust, cost, or drawdown risk.

## 13. Human-Brain-Like Concepts

The Brain preserves many cognitive and neuroscience-inspired concepts. These are not claims that the system is conscious or biological. They are design inspirations or functional analogs unless explicitly defined otherwise.

### Predictive processing

The Brain predicts what should happen, compares predictions with actual input, and updates when surprised.

### Active inference / free-energy principle

The Brain can be framed as reducing uncertainty through perception and action. It seeks information or action that makes the world more predictable and valuable.

### Global workspace / blackboard

Many modules may process information in parallel, but only the most important items enter a shared workspace where they influence broader decision-making.

### Reinforcement learning

Actions that lead to good outcomes are reinforced. Actions that lead to pain are weakened.

### Hebbian learning

Things that repeatedly occur together become more strongly connected.

### Anti-Hebbian learning

False associations are weakened when co-occurrence does not predict value.

### Attractor networks

Stable patterns can pull new information toward familiar interpretations. This is useful for recognition but risky for bias.

### Sparse distributed representations

Concepts can be represented by patterns across many nodes rather than one isolated label.

### Theory of mind

The Brain models what other people may know, want, fear, believe, and respond to.

### Embodiment and affordances

Even without a physical body, the Brain needs a model of its environment, constraints, capabilities, and possible actions.

### Dreaming and replay

Offline processing can recombine old memories, search for patterns, test scenarios, and find missed connections.

### Self-model and metacognition

The Brain should track what it knows, does not know, may be wrong about, and how reliable its own reasoning is.

### Consciousness-adjacent models

The corpus includes consciousness-related theories as reference models. These should not be treated as proof that the Brain has subjective experience, qualia, sentience, or phenomenal consciousness.

## 18. Roadmap Stages

### V0: Proof harness

Build the smallest executable loop: fixture input, scoring, opportunity, candidate action, approval gate, simulated outcome, reward/pain learning, replay, acceptance report.

### V1: Money spine

Build the commercial intelligence system: source priority, signal scoring, opportunity scoring, outcome attribution, reward/pain, graph updates, dashboards.

### V2: Advanced cognition

Add richer memory, analogy, counterfactuals, truth maintenance, social cognition, confidence calibration, and stronger internal debate.

### V3: Digital organism

Add deeper self-modeling, identity continuity, offline dreaming, global workspace, developmental learning, embodiment analogs, and adaptive strategy mutation.

### V4: Research / consciousness-adjacent expansion

Preserve and explore advanced theories such as integrated information theory references, attention schema theory, active inference, free-energy principle, spiking/neuromorphic analogs, cognitive architectures, and stronger consciousness measurement boundaries.

## 21. Glossary

**Brain:** The full compiled intelligence/cognition/commercial operating system described by the corpus.

**Money spine:** The loop that learns which signals create money and which waste time.

**Signal:** A piece of observed change or information.

**Evidence:** The part of a signal that supports or contradicts a claim.

**Belief:** A live claim with confidence, evidence, contradiction, and update history.

**Attention score:** A score estimating whether a signal deserves processing.

**Reward:** A positive learning signal from useful outcome.

**Pain:** A negative learning signal from false positives, wasted time, trust risk, or loss.

**Attribution:** The process of deciding what caused an outcome.

**Graph rewiring:** Strengthening, weakening, creating, pruning, merging, or splitting nodes and edges based on evidence and outcomes.

**Formula trace:** The record showing which formula produced a score and from what inputs.

**Approval gate:** Human control point before external consequence.

**V0:** Minimal proof harness. Not the total Brain.

**V1/V2/V3/V4:** Later roadmap stages for money spine, advanced cognition, digital organism, and research/consciousness-adjacent layers.

**Preserve by default:** Rule that material remains in the corpus unless an allowed rejection reason applies.
